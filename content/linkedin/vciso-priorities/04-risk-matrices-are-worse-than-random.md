Hubbard and Seiersen, in *How to Measure Anything in Cybersecurity Risk*, catalog three independent research streams against ordinal risk matrices.

The psychology of scales. Budescu's climate scientists and Heuer's intelligence analysts interpreted the same verbal labels across ranges spanning orders of magnitude. The mathematics. Cox's "risk matrix theorem" proved that risk matrices can be worse than random under certain mathematically provable conditions. The same input risk can map to different cells. Different risks can map to the same cell. The empirical studies. Hubbard and Evans found that 76 percent of ordinal responses cluster on two values, reducing a 5x5 matrix to effectively 2x2.

The replacement is calibrated estimation. Untrained experts, including cybersecurity professionals, are systematically overconfident. Calibration training moves them from 90 percent confidence intervals that capture the true value 40 percent of the time to intervals that capture it 80 to 90 percent of the time.

The governance frameworks do not engage with this. ISO 31010 calls risk matrices "strongly applicable." NIST 800-30 uses likelihood and impact scales. The frameworks assume ordinal methods work because they have always assumed ordinal methods work.
